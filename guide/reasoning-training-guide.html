the contribution · §1

What the paper actually delivers.

Before R1, "reasoning models" like OpenAI-o1 were closed — you could use them, not study them. DeepSeek-R1's contribution is open weights and an open recipe: a way to grow o1-level reasoning out of a base model, driven mostly by reinforcement learning rather than human-written reasoning traces.

deliverable 1

R1-Zero

Reasoning learned by pure RL on the base model — no supervised fine-tuning at all. Proof that RL alone can incentivize reasoning.

deliverable 2

DeepSeek-R1

A multi-stage pipeline (SFT + RL, repeated) that keeps R1-Zero's reasoning but fixes readability and aligns to human preferences. ≈ OpenAI-o1-1217.

deliverable 3

Distilled models

The reasoning poured into small dense models (Qwen & Llama, 1.5B–70B) by SFT — beating far larger instruction-tuned models on math.

"…we build upon DeepSeek-V3-Base and employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as our RL framework. The reward signal is solely based on the correctness of final predictions against ground-truth answers… Notably, we bypass the conventional supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase before RL training." — §1

The bet: don't teach the model how to reason — reward it only for getting the answer right, and let good reasoning emerge on its own. Skipping SFT is deliberate: human-written reasoning patterns might cap what the model discovers. Give it hard problems, a reliable checker, and compute — and the reasoning grows.

the core idea · why "reasoning" at all

A reasoning model changes the output contract.

A plain LLM maps a question straight to an answer. A reasoning model first writes a chain of intermediate tokens — a scratchpad — and only then commits. R1 wraps that scratchpad in <think>…</think> tags.

question
17 × 24 = ?
→
reasoning chain
<think> 17×20+17×4 </think>
→
answer
<answer> 408 </answer>
intuition 1

Decomposition

Break a hard problem into easier sub-problems the model has already seen. Not 17×24 in one leap — 17×20 + 17×4 = …

intuition 2

More tokens = more compute

Every token is another forward pass. Answer in one token → one pass to decide. Think for 30 tokens first → 30 extra passes before committing. Reasoning tokens literally buy compute — that's test-time compute.

deliverable 1 · §2 · the boldest result

DeepSeek-R1-Zero — reasoning from pure RL.

Take DeepSeek-V3-Base. Skip supervised fine-tuning entirely. Just run GRPO with a reward that checks the final answer and the format. No human reasoning traces, no value network. And it works — reasoning behavior emerges.

what emerges, unprompted

Self-verification & longer thinking

With only a correctness signal, the model starts to re-check its work, reflect, and explore alternatives — and its responses grow longer over training, because thinking longer earns more reward. Nobody set a length target.

the famous moment

The "aha moment"

Mid-solution, an intermediate R1-Zero literally writes "Wait, wait. That's an aha moment" and re-derives its answer. Self-correction was discovered by the reward, not taught.

"Although DeepSeek-R1-Zero demonstrates excellent reasoning capabilities, it faces challenges such as poor readability and language mixing, occasionally combining English and Chinese within a single chain-of-thought response." — §1

Pure RL optimizes for being right, not for being read. So R1-Zero's scratchpads come out messy — tangled formatting, and English and Chinese mixed in one thought. It's a fantastic reasoner and an awkward assistant. That gap is the entire reason DeepSeek-R1 exists: keep the reasoning, add readability and alignment.

R1-Zero's two problems → the to-do list for R1 1 · Readability: the reasoning is hard to follow.   2 · Language mixing: it switches languages mid-thought. The next sections fix both — without losing the reasoning RL taught it.
the engine · §2.1 · GRPO

GRPO — reward what works, with no critic.

The whole trick in one line: ask the same question several times, and reward the answers that beat the group's own average. No value network, no human labels — just a function that checks the answer.

1 · SAMPLE
Ask G times
Draw G completions from the current model for one prompt (here G=4).
2 · SCORE
Rule-based reward
Cheap checker: right answer? correct format? No learned reward model.
3 · ADVANTAGE
z-score vs. group
(r − mean) / std. Above average → positive. Below → negative.
4 · NUDGE
REINFORCE
−(logprob · adv). Push toward winners, away from losers.

the entire GRPO update
def grpo_step(prompt_str, G=4):
    completions, logps, rewards = [], [], []
    for _ in range(G):                          # 1. sample G completions
        full, lp = R.sample_completion(model, prompt_ids)
        rewards.append(R.reward(prompt_str, comp))   # 2. rule-based score
        logps.append(lp)
    r   = torch.tensor(rewards)
    adv = (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + 1e-6)       # 3. group-relative advantage (z-score)
    loss = -(torch.cat(logps) * adv).mean()      # 4. REINFORCE toward winners
    return loss

Why no critic? PPO vs. GRPO

Classic RLHF (PPO) trains a second network — a value model — to judge each state. GRPO drops it and uses the group's mean reward as the baseline. That's why the paper adopts it: less memory, simpler, cheaper.

PPO — classic RLHF

  • Baseline: a learned value network (extra NN)
  • Networks trained: policy + value
  • Memory: ~2×
  • Reward: usually a learned reward model — hackable

GRPO — DeepSeek-R1

  • Baseline: mean reward of the group
  • Networks trained: policy only
  • Memory: ~1×
  • Reward: rule-based checker — unhackable
the fuel · §2.2 & §3 · verifiable rewards

Rule-based rewards — the signal that can't be hacked.

GRPO is only as good as its reward. R1's core insight: for math and code you don't need a learned reward model — you can check the answer with code. That signal is cheap, and the policy can't fool it. Rewards stack up as the pipeline progresses:

R1-Zero & onward

Accuracy

+1 if the final answer is correct, checked against ground truth — math results, unit tests, compiler/exec for code. Pure verification.

R1-Zero & onward

Format

Reward for putting the reasoning between <think> tags and the answer where the checker expects it. Makes outputs gradeable.

added in R1

Language consistency

The fraction of the CoT in the target language. Directly attacks R1-Zero's language mixing — at a small hit to raw accuracy, but far more readable.

RL from verifiable rewards — the big idea If you can check an answer with code — math, unit tests, a game score — you can turn that check into a reward and improve the model from its own samples. Cheap, unhackable, and still how frontier reasoning models are trained today. For the final RL stage, R1 adds preference reward models for helpfulness and harmlessness on top of the rules.

"…we introduce a language consistency reward during RL training, which is calculated as the proportion of target language words in the CoT. Although ablation experiments show that such alignment results in a slight degradation in the model's performance, this reward aligns with human preferences, making it more readable." — §3

A rare, honest trade-off: adding the language reward makes the model slightly less accurate but much nicer to read. The team chose readability. It's a reminder that a "reward function" isn't neutral — it encodes what you value, and you can dial in behavior by adding terms to it.

the honest part · §5 · limitations

What the paper says R1 still can't do.

A good paper lists its own failures. R1's authors are candid — these are the stated limitations, straight from the paper.

limitation

Structured output & tool use

Structured-output quality lags existing models, and R1 can't call tools (search, calculators) to boost its answers.

limitation

Language mixing

Tuned for Chinese & English. Queries in other languages can still trigger mixing — likely inherited from the base checkpoint.

limitation

Prompt sensitivity

Few-shot prompting consistently hurts. The paper recommends a zero-shot setting: describe the problem, specify the output format.

limitation

Software engineering

Long eval times made large-scale RL impractical here, so R1 shows little gain over DeepSeek-V3 on SWE benchmarks.

limitation

Token efficiency / overthinking

R1 allocates tokens by difficulty, but still overthinks simple questions — room to spend compute more frugally.

the deeper challenge

Reward hacking

Pure RL needs a reliable reward. For fuzzy tasks (e.g. writing) a learned RM gets gamed — so those still lean on human-annotated SFT.

the boundary of the method The through-line: RL from verifiable rewards is only as strong as the verifier. Where you can check the answer with code, R1's recipe shines. Where you can't, you're back to human data and hackable reward models — the open frontier.
